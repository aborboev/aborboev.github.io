Personal project · 2026

4-DOF desktop arm.
Math first, then CAD.

Kinematics and dynamics in MATLAB, built on real mass properties from SolidWorks, drove every design change.

The approach

Before building anything, I wrote the math that describes how the arm moves and what loads it sees, in MATLAB, using the real weights of my SolidWorks parts.

What it changed

I mapped everywhere the arm can reach, how hard the motors work at each point, and when it would tip over. That told me where to cut weight and which motors to buy.

The result

−30%load on the shoulder motor from the first arm link, after cutting out excess material

Bio/Materials Tribology Lab · UF

Needle guide.

A printed V-cradle that keeps the needle straight on its way into the sample. Next up: redesigning the rig's entire hardware system.

The problem

In hydrogel insertion tests, the needle bent sideways before entering the sample, which skewed the friction and force data.

What I did

Designed the guide in SolidWorks, then printed and iterated it on the rig. I also analyze the test data in R, turning raw force-vs-time runs into plots and summary tables for a paper in progress.

Estimated result

~20%less needle deflection during insertion

EML2322L · Design and Manufacturing Lab

Air-powered mixer.
Built for $65.

Designed under a hard budget, then machined in the shop. It runs at 350–400 rpm.

The design

Two-belt drive through a jackshaft, with step pulleys for two mixer speeds (about 267 and 133 rpm from a 400 rpm engine) and an idler tensioner. Belt lengths and tensioner placement worked out by hand.

The build

Machined the air engine on the mill, lathe, and CNC, with every part designed for the shop's capabilities. The finished engine runs at 350–400 rpm.

Budget

$65total, working mixer delivered
